Weather Station and Micro-Climate Sensor Plan

Companion to the site climate page, which notes that the lot has no thermometer yet, and the water-harvesting plan, which calls for a rain gauge. Prices, specifications, and range figures come from Ecowitt's store pages and manuals and from the other sellers' pages linked below, and they will drift, so follow the links before ordering. This page summarizes them in its own words and links to the sellers; it does not copy their catalogs. The Pick in Brief

Buy the Wittboy GW3001 kit ($204.99): a GW3000 gateway and a WS90 outdoor array. It suits a remote, Starlink-connected site better than anything else in Ecowitt's lineup. It costs $5 more than the older GW2001 kit ($199.99), whose gateway lacks the SD slot.

  • Gateway (GW3000). Wi-Fi and Ethernet, a microSD slot that logs readings as CSV files you can download from its web page or API, a replaceable external radio antenna, and USB-C power. The GW2000 in the GW2001 kit lists no SD slot. A local copy means a Starlink or Wi-Fi outage does not leave a hole in the frost record.
  • Array (WS90). Wind speed, wind direction, rain, temperature, humidity, light, and UV in one solar-powered unit with no moving parts.
  • Room to grow. One gateway takes up to 8 temperature/humidity sensors, 8 temperature probes, 8 leaf-wetness, 16 soil, 4 leak, 4 laser-distance, and 4 PM2.5 sensors, a lightning sensor, and 16 water timers, valves, or smart plugs.
  • Pricier units add a screen or a modem, not capability. The Wittboy Pro HP2564 ($299.99) is the same WS90 with a 7-inch touchscreen console and a shorter add-on list (no irrigation timers). The WittField WS6210 (from $329.99) is a 4G and Wi-Fi receiver for sites without Wi-Fi and needs its own sensors; Starlink makes it unnecessary.
  • Nothing to port-forward. The gateway sends its readings out to ecowitt.net, so Starlink's lack of inbound connections does not matter.

How It Measures Wind and Direction

The gateway has no wind sensor. It receives readings by radio from the array outdoors (915 MHz, about every 9 seconds) and passes them along: WS90 → GW3000 → Wi-Fi or Ethernet → Starlink → Ecowitt app and website, with a copy on the SD card.

  • No cups, no vane. The WS90's ultrasonic anemometer sends sound pulses between transducers. Wind speeds up or slows the pulses, and the electronics work out speed and direction from the timing.
  • What it reports. 0 to 89 mph (40 m/s), within ±2 mph below 22 mph and ±10% above, starting at about 1 mph; direction within ±15° in 1° steps; gust is the highest speed in the last 28 seconds.
  • Aim it. Point the array's NORTH arrow due north (WS90 manual), keep the pole vertical and the array level, and check the arrow again as the last step before tightening the bolts. The fences run within 0.2° of true north, so sight along the west fence; a phone compass reads magnetic north, which is well off true north here.
  • Winter. Use lithium AAs; the manual warns against alkaline cells, especially with the heater on. The optional 12 V heater cord (10 or 20 m) warms only the wind-measurement area. The rain sensor on top is not heated, and the pole must be within reach of 12 V power.
  • Cups and a vane instead. The GW3002 kit ($118.99) pairs the GW3000 with the WS69: moving parts, no heater cord, but a listed ±0.3 °C temperature accuracy against ±1 °C for the WS90. The WS90 is still the better fit here.

Freeze protection with the AC1100 (red shed and pump house)

Yes. In the Ecowitt app, a Smart rule can use a WN31 channel's temperature as its trigger and switch the plug on or off, so the indoor sensors (S and P) can run a small heater or warming pad. The rules run in the gateway, so a Starlink outage does not matter. The sensor and the plug must be on the same gateway (the AC1100 manual says rules cannot yet use another gateway's sensors); here that is the GW3000 in the shed. Two plugs cost $59.98.

  • Settings. The app lists each WN31 channel as "Temp and Humidity CH_: Temperature," with "is less than" and "is greater than" tests. The WN31 reads within about ±2 °F every 61 seconds, so 38 °F leaves margin above freezing and the 7 °F gap prevents cycling.
  • Rating. The plug is rated 12 A (1,440 W at 120 V) and cuts the load off itself only at 18 A, so a 1,500 W space heater (12.5 A) is over the rating and nothing would stop it. Keep the load under about 1,000 W, plugged straight into the AC1100 and then into the wall outlet (GFCI in the pump house), with no extension cord.
  • Choose a heater that restarts and has its own thermostat. A heater or pad with a knob thermostat heats as soon as power returns and holds its own set point (about 40 °F) if the radio or gateway fails; many digital or remote-control heaters and electric blankets stay off after a power cut. Each thermostat covers the other: if the heater's sticks on, the rule cuts the plug above 45 °F.
  • Where it can fail. The chain is WN31, radio, gateway, radio, plug. Ecowitt's "communication unstable" alert disables Smart rules, and the shed gateway is rated only to 14 °F, so do not rely on the plug alone for the well pump. The 35 °F alert warns you if the heat did not keep up.
  • Energy. If the pump house runs from the battery bank, a 100 W pad on for half of a 14-hour night uses about 0.7 kWh, and a 750 W heater on the same schedule about 5 kWh. Warm the pump or pressure tank directly rather than the room.
  • Not published. Ecowitt gives no operating-temperature or weatherproof rating for the AC1100 and does not say what state it takes after a power cut. Mount it dry and warm, and test both before winter; the app shows its signal and live watts.

Irrigation Tank: Auto-Fill and Level Management

The site data lists a 3,000 gal black poly tank beside the pump house, about 8 ft across and 8 ft tall, so each inch of depth holds about 31 gal. A straight-sided tank that size holds about 3,008 gal brim-full, so the level at the overflow fitting is a little lower: measure it. The site data does not say how the tank is filled today (the pump, the pipe size, the inlet and overflow heights); check those before buying. The LDS01 laser and the WFC02 valve reach the gateway by 915 MHz radio, not Wi-Fi. The tank is about 164 ft from the shed, inside the 328 ft rating, but it sits beside the pump house and the steel solar container, so test the signal at the valve's real spot.

Your idea works on paper: a daily timer opens the valve, the laser stops it when the tank is full, and the same rule shuts it again if the tank is still full the next day. The timer and a stop-on-sensor rule are documented. What Ecowitt does not say is whether the laser's level can be the trigger that stops the valve, so there is a plan for each answer.

Plan A: the laser stops the valve If the app offers the laser as a trigger

  1. Timer. In the valve's Plan screen, open it at 100% every day, at a time the well pump can run, for a capped time (see Values to set).
  2. Stop rule. In Smart, add the trigger "laser depth is at least FULL" (or "air gap is at most …," whichever the app lists) with the action Water Valve: Off. Set the effective period to all day, every day.
  3. Still full the next day. The timer opens the valve and the rule should shut it again within about 90 seconds (one laser report, about 80 seconds, plus the 7 seconds the valve takes to close), costing up to about 19 gal at 13 gpm.
  4. Prove it once. With the tank at FULL, start a 3-minute manual run (Duration) in the app. If the valve shuts within about 90 seconds, the rule fires again and Plan A works; the Smart log lists each firing. If it runs the full 3 minutes, the rule fires only when the level crosses FULL, so use Plan B's cap.

Plan B: timer and meter only Works whatever the app allows

  1. Timer by volume. The 1 in valve with the flow meter can run a Plan by volume: your daily use plus about 20%, with a time cap as the second limit. The manual's screens enter volume in liters (1 gal = 3.8 L).
  2. Laser as the gauge. Its low and high alerts tell you when the numbers are wrong, and the valve's log reports each day's gallons, so retune the volume monthly.
  3. Float valve as the stop. A mechanical float valve on the inlet is the final shut-off whatever the electronics do.
  4. A script is a project, not a plan. A program on your network could read the laser and command the valve, but Ecowitt's local API document (August 2024) covers only the WFC01 and AC1100.

Install and protect

  • Laser. A 45 to 50 mm hole in the lid on a level surface (a flat hatch or a level plate, if the lid is domed), the bubble level centered, 40 mm of clear space in front of the lens, and at least 3.4 in from the tank wall (Ecowitt's rule is 3.5 cm per 100 cm of height). Keep the beam clear of the inlet stream, pipes, and any float arm. Ecowitt notes extra jitter when the air gap is about 1.1 to 1.5 m, which centering the bubble usually cures. The display and battery box connect by 1.5 m cables, so they need not sit on the lid; the sensor is rated to 122 °F and a black tank in summer sun can run hotter than the air.
  • Valve. On the supply line with the meter's arrow along the flow, thread tape on every joint, and the actuator upright. End the inlet above the water line (an air gap, so the tank cannot siphon back) and fit an overflow at least as large as the inlet, aimed away from the pump house and containers. If the fill line comes straight off the well pump, check that the pump has pressure control so a closed valve cannot dead-head it.
  • Backstops. The timer keeps running if the radio link drops, but the stop rule does not: "communication unstable" disables Smart rules. A low battery locks the valve and closes it if it is open. Keep the float valve and overflow as the last line of defense.
  • Keeping it running. Both devices take 8 AA alkaline cells; change them each spring (the valve lasts over 10 months at one cycle a day). Compare the laser's Depth with a tape reading each month, and watch its heater counter: more than about 30 a day means a dirty or fogging lens. In fall, close the supply, drain the valve and meter (rated above 32 °F), and take the batteries out of both.

Before You Order

  • Cold. The GW3000 is rated down to 14 °F (−10 °C). The coldest nights average −11 °F, and the record is −32 °F (data/climate.json, Prairie SNOTEL). Unless the shed stays above 14 °F, keep the gateway in a heated spot or an insulated box with a small thermostat-controlled heater, and run its antenna outside. The freeze alerts for the buildings depend on this gateway, Wi-Fi, and power, and the gateway's own indoor reading stops at 14 °F. An AC1100 smart plug can switch a heater in each building from the indoor sensors' readings. The WS90, WH52, and WN31 transmit down to −40 °F; the LDS01 is rated only to −4 °F.
  • Data. ecowitt.net keeps day-by-day detail for 3 months, weekly data for 1 year, monthly for 2 years, and yearly for 4. The SD card writes CSV files in its root folder. It must be FAT32 (cards over 32 GB need reformatting with a tool such as Rufus), can be swapped without a restart, and is managed from the gateway's web page (GW3000 manual). Back up the CSV files monthly. The repository only needs the daily lows and highs, kept apart from the SNOTEL record, since on-site readings are not station readings.
  • Setup. Use two lithium AAs in the WS90, run it for a week at a temporary spot, and aim its north arrow. In the gateway's Device Settings (GW3000 manual), turn on Temperature Compensation to reduce sun heating of the WS90's temperature. Check each temperature sensor in an ice bath (32 °F) before it goes outside, and note any offset.
  • Soil sensors and rain gauges. The WH52 (sites 1 to 4) goes in the basin of the nearest tree, ideally one used for the perc tests. Put the CoCoRaHS gauge and the WH40H on separate posts a few feet from the WS90, clear of the trees.
  • Accuracy. The WS90's temperature sensor is not fan-ventilated, so it can read warm at midday in calm sun; that matters less on frost nights. The WN31 and WS90 are both listed at ±1 °C. Neither the WS90's rain sensor nor the WH40H measures snow, so use the CoCoRaHS gauge and the Prairie SNOTEL for winter precipitation.
  • Starlink. The gateway joins 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only. If it will not connect, temporarily split the 2.4 and 5 GHz networks in the Starlink app (a third-party how-to), or use the Ethernet port.
  • Wi-Fi at the shed. The shed is about … ft from the pump house. The GW3000 lists 30 m (98 ft) of Wi-Fi range, so check the signal at the shed with a phone, and use the gateway's Ethernet port or a mesh node if it is weak. The GW3000 manual also describes moving the antenna on an extension cable to the best-signal spot; check that the cable is in the box.
  • Still unknown. Whether the shed and pump house stay above freezing, and the shed above 14 °F (the indoor sensors will show it), whether Wi-Fi reaches the north orchard, how far the radio really carries on this ground and through the pump house walls, how the tank is filled today, and whether the laser's level can stop the valve (the Ecowitt question in the tank section). The range test settles the radio question.
