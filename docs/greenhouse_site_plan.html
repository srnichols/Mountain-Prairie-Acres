Rules, Approvals, and Safety

  • Building permit. Idaho Code 39-4116(5) tells counties to exempt agricultural buildings from the state building codes, and its list includes "horticultural structures, including detached production greenhouses and crop protection shelters." It excludes places of human habitation, workplaces where farm products are processed or packaged, and places used by the public. A county may still issue a permit to check road setbacks and utility easements, for no more than its cost. Elmore County's permit page lists its own exempt work (sheds under 200 sq ft, fences, retaining walls under 4 ft unless supporting surcharge, tanks under 5,000 gal) and does not mention greenhouses, so get the exemption confirmed in writing. The ft retaining wall at the berm's foot is under that 4 ft line, but it holds a slope that climbs another ft above it, which is the kind of surcharge the list leaves out, so ask about the wall in the same letter. With no code review, the pit's shoring and roof are the owner's own engineering.
  • Snow and wind. The county's maps put Prairie in the lb/sq ft ground snow load zone and set a mph design wind for houses (see site baselines; the county required a neighbor's building to be designed for the snow figure). An exempt greenhouse is not held to them, but they are an honest measure of what it will see. Ask the vendor what ground snow load and wind speed a 76 ft run is designed for.
  • Before digging. Call 811 two to ten business days ahead (Idaho Code 55-2205); the marks last four weeks, and the call must be repeated if digging has not begun within three. OSHA's excavation rules require cave-in protection at 5 ft deep (29 CFR 1926.652), a ladder or stair within 25 ft of anyone in a trench 4 ft deep or more, and spoil and equipment at least 2 ft back from the edge (1926.651); its maximum slopes by soil type are 3/4:1, 1:1, and 1½:1. Those are rules for employers, but the physics is the same for anyone, and a 6 ft wall of soil can fall faster than anyone can climb out.
  • Grant. The vendor says its kit qualifies for the USDA NRCS EQIP high-tunnel grant, about $8,000 for 1,000 sq ft. From a web search, and not confirmed with NRCS: payments in Idaho are capped near 2,178 sq ft (this tunnel is ), only unheated tunnels qualify, and the contract has to be approved before anything is bought or dug. The pit is not a kit and probably does not qualify. Ask your USDA Service Center what records and deadlines apply.

Open Questions

  1. EQIP or self-funded? It sets the timing (apply first) and keeps the tunnel unheated.
  2. The retaining wall. The wall at the berm's foot is about ft long and 3 ft high, and the slope it holds climbs another ft above it. What is it made of (interlocking blocks with geogrid, poured concrete, or gabions), who designs it, and does Elmore County treat it as exempt? Its exempt list says "retaining walls under 4 feet (unless supporting surcharge)," and the sloping soil above this wall is likely to count. See the pit page.
  3. Which end gets the wide door? The gothic page now draws the wide center door the vendor shows at both ends, so a cart can run straight through the 76 ft, door to door; only carts go inside, no tractor. The west door faces the alley, and its path from the yard goes around the pit; the east door faces the yard side over open ground. Does each end need the wide door, or can one end keep the standard 80 in door if the cart fits it?
