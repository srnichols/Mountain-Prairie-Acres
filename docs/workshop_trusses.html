Workshop Pole Barn: Truss Package

The brief for the truss plant · 32 × 48 ft gambrel attic trusses with a connected loft floor · Prairie, Elmore County, Idaho · planned for 2028

The package: Overview · Specs · Plans and elevations · Sections and details · Trusses · Materials and cost

Every number here comes from the same design model as the rest of the package. Nothing is ordered or permitted yet; an Idaho-licensed engineer confirms every load and dimension.

What We Are Asking the Truss Plant For

  1. Design and seal 25 gambrel attic trusses with a connected loft floor at 2′-0″ OC, numbered T1 at the west gable to T25 at the east: each bottom chord is a loft joist spanning the full 32′-0″ from wall line to wall line. 23 are common and 2 are gable-end (T1 and T25).
  2. Keep the loft room open and as tall as the shape allows: no webs or posts in the 18′-0″ between the two pitch-break lines, with 7′-1″ clear at its edges, crossed only by a collar tie on T1 to T20 with its underside 8′-0″ above the loft floor (headroom). Brace the rest of the truss in the knee zones and with plates (bracing and blocking).
  3. Keep the roof shape: the pitch break and the ridge stay put (the county's 27 ft peak limit leaves 8″ to spare), and the underside of the floor stays at 13′-2″ for the door below (profile).
  4. Design for 120 psf ground snow, 115 mph wind, and a 40 psf loft floor (loads).
  5. Land on the carriers: (3) 1¾″ × 9¼″ LVL bolted to the inside face of every post, the wall plate (bearing).
  6. Build in the five special cases: the stair opening, the hoist beam, the loft doors, the loft window, and the gable-end trusses (special trusses).
  7. Send back sealed drawings, the reactions, the clear headroom your design gives, and a quote with the boom-truck delivery as its own line (what to send back).

Bracing and Blocking

The owner wants a collar tie across the upper slopes to help the roof carry the 120 psf snow, and it is in the design (J): it is the one member that crosses the open room. Besides it, the pitch break (the knee) is the joint that works hardest, so we expect bracing there. Webs, kickers, blocking, and plates cost the open room nothing when they stay in the knee zones or lie flat on the faces of the chords, so please use them first; the profile shows one way, marked K and L. Steps 1 to 6 are in the order we prefer them: send the first design that works, say which steps it uses, and price the later ones as alternates.

Headroom and Walking Space

The loft is a room people walk and work in, and the profile gives the headroom this roof shape allows under the owner's collar tie. We ask the plant to protect it.

  • Clear height is measured from the top of the loft floor up to the underside of the top chords, and under the collar tie to the underside of the tie. The plans page measures headroom to the top of the chords (the roof line), which is 7½″ higher at the ridge and 11″ higher at the pitch break.
  • The roof line is fixed. Do not raise the ridge or the pitch break. The roof line may rise near the wall for the heel.
  • The clear heights below are the least we accept, measured under 7¼″ top chords. If your loads need deeper chords, tell us how many inches deeper and what the clear heights become: each extra inch of chord costs about an inch of headroom, and more at the pitch break.
  • The room stays open between the pitch-break lines: no webs or posts. The collar tie on T1 to T20 and the hoist beam at the east end (S2) are the only things in it, so the tallest headroom is 8′-0″, under the tie. The rest of the bracing goes in the knee zones and on the faces of the chords; only the last step in bracing and blocking comes into the room.
  • If the open room cannot be built at 24″ OC for these loads, send the closest design that can, and say what it gives up. The steps in bracing and blocking are in the order we prefer them. Price each one.

What to Send Back

  1. Sealed truss drawings by an Idaho-licensed engineer, for each truss type: common, gable-end, the stair truss (T2), and the hoist trusses (T21 to T25), with member sizes, lumber grades, plate sizes, and the loads you used.
  2. A placement plan with the web layout, the permanent lateral bracing and bridging you require (including the solid blocking between trusses and any bracing the collar ties need), and the purlin spacing your top chords assume.
  3. Reactions, vertical and uplift, at every bearing, for each truss type, so the engineer can size the carriers, their bolts, the posts, and the hoist anchors, plus the hurricane ties you specify (we assume H2.5A, 2 per bearing).
  4. The clear headroom your design gives: at the room's edge, under the collar tie, at the ridge, and the room width at 7′ and 5′ clear. If your top chords are deeper than 7¼″, say how much. Say which steps of bracing and blocking your design uses.
  5. The collar tie you design for T1 to T20: its size and grade, the end plates and connections, the pull it carries under the snow, and how you carry the upper chords of T21 to T25, which take none.
  6. The heel block and plates you propose (we draw a 2×6 block), the tail, and the post-top cut they need.
  7. The snow cases you used: drift and unbalanced snow at the pitch break.
  8. A quote by line: common trusses with their collar ties, gable-end trusses, the special trusses (T2 and T21 to T25), any connectors you supply, and the alternates: a 60 psf loft floor, 16″ OC, and any step of bracing and blocking beyond the knee zones (the kicker across the knee).
  9. Delivery by boom truck, as its own line: the delivery fee, the boom-truck time and the hours included on site, and whether the driver can set the bundles on the walls (the walls stand 14′-0″ above the main floor). We will confirm the route onto the pad before the delivery date.
  10. Lead time from signed drawings to delivery, and how long the quote holds.
  11. Anything you cannot meet: name the requirement and propose the closest design.
