Pit Greenhouse: Hybrid 20 × 36 ft

An unheated, earth-sheltered greenhouse sunk 6 ft into the ground south of the gothic tunnel and centered on it, in cell B14 · Oehler's post, shoring, and polyethylene method, scaled up · planned for 2028 or 2029 · a draft for review

This page redraws the owner's two "hybrid 20 × 36" drawings (a floor plan and a side profile, 2026), which stretch the "Large Greenhouse" in Mike Oehler's The Earth-Sheltered Solar Greenhouse Book (second edition, Mole Publishing Company, pages 204 and 205) from a 16 × 16 ft room to 20 × 36 ft. The book's pages are not reproduced here; the drawings are new. Where the redraw differs from the owner's drawings, the change table says so. For where it sits on the lot, how the water runs, and what the berm needs, see the site plan; for the tunnel it is centered on, the gothic tunnel page.

The Design in Brief

  • Room: inside the posts. From the north wall: a 2 ft strip for water drums, two growing beds each 15 × 12 ft, a 2 ft walk, then a 4 ft cold zone along the south wall. Walks run between the beds (2 ft) and along the east and west walls (2 ft).
  • Depths below the original grade: beds ft (the soil is left in place), walks ft, the cold zone's floor ft, post bottoms ft. The owner chose 6 ft for the cold zone in front and 4 ft for the beds; the cold zone's floor is , and a bed stands ft above the walk beside it.
  • Posts: 4 ft apart both ways, , charred and wrapped in plastic where they stand in the ground, with shoring boards and polyethylene holding back the cut earth.
  • Roof: one slope facing south, rigid poly glazing panels (the owner's drawing says "hard-ridge poly panels"), rising 10 ft over a 19.5 ft run (), from ft above grade at the south eave to ft at the north wall.
  • North: an earth berm up to the roof line, so only the 4 ft vent posts stand clear of the ground ( ft above grade). A ft retaining wall holds the berm's foot, so its slopes stop at the wall instead of tapering all the way to the ground: the berm is ft deep north to south instead of , and takes about cu yd less soil.
  • Unheated: the earth around it, the water drums, and the low cold zone do the work; no heater is planned.

The Roof Slope and Winter Sun

The 27° roof is a compromise between the light it catches in winter and how tall the earthwork must be. The table shows the share of the direct sunbeam a south-facing surface catches at solar noon (the climate page's noon sun heights for the lot), before glazing losses; a steeper face catches more in winter and less in summer.

Roof or wall tiltDec 21 noonMar 21 noonJun 21 noonNorth end above grade over the same 19.5 ft run

At 27° the roof catches about of the December beam and at the equinox. A 45° roof would catch in December but would lift the north end about ft above grade, nearly doubling the berm's height and so several times its soil. Keeping 27° and the 10.5 ft berm is a reasonable middle, and the owner's drawings already assume it.

Items to Settle

The entry, and a second way out

  • The south wall stands only 0.5 ft above grade, so a door cannot go in it. The stair needs a hatch or a raised frame at the top, with a curb and a drain, because that spot is uphill.
  • Snow slides south off a 27° roof, straight onto that stair. Either the entry moves to the downhill west end, or the top of the stair gets a pitched cover that throws the snow clear.
  • A room ft deep should have two exits. The east door at walk level is drawn as optional; it should be built, which means a ramp or stair cut outside it.

Posts inside the room

  • The book's 16 × 16 ft plan puts posts only around the edge. At 20 × 36 ft, the owner's 4 ft grid puts posts in the beds, walks, and sink.
  • Beams that span north to south from the wall posts, or fewer, stouter interior posts, would free the beds. Roof framing at 4 ft centers has not been checked against snow; the county's lb/sq ft zone is unconfirmed for the lot.

Water, drainage, and rock

  • The floor cannot drain by gravity; plan a gravel sump in the sandy loam and perc-test it first. Divert surface water around the south and east sides (see the site plan).
  • The owner reports rock about ft down, ft below the post bottoms. A test hole to about ft comes before any design is final.
  • An unheated, closed room will collect moisture: plan a high vent (the roof vent) and a low opening (the east door) for summer air.

Heat, mass, and the ends

  • Nine standing drums hold gal, about Btu for each degree of temperature change. More mass means a second layer on a proper rack; a full 55 gal drum weighs about 460 lb.
  • The east and west walls rise from 0.5 ft to 10.5 ft above grade, about sq ft each. Glazed, insulated, or banked with earth? Banking adds soil (see the site plan).
  • The cold zone's floor is and only ft below the bed tops; the owner's first drawings had the sink 5 ft below them. Cold air still settles at the lowest floor, but the beds now sit closer to that pool: a judgment, not a calculation. Sensors at bed height and on the cold zone's floor through the first winter will show whether it matters.

The berm and its retaining wall

  • The load. The ft wall holds a slope that climbs another ft above it, so it carries more than the same wall would with level soil behind it. This page does not size it. Interlocking blocks with geogrid, poured concrete, and stone-filled gabions are the usual ways to build one; each needs a base prepared to its own rules, and the county's in frost depth decides how deep a rigid footing goes.
  • The permit. Elmore County's exempt-work list reads "Retaining walls under 4 feet (unless supporting surcharge)". The wall is under 4 ft, but the sloping earth above it is the kind of surcharge that exception leaves out. Ask the county in writing whether the wall needs a permit or an engineer's design, along with the question about the greenhouse itself.
  • Drainage. Gravel and a perforated drain pipe behind the wall, running out to daylight (the ground falls to the northwest), keep water from building up against it. Keep the toe swale out from its face; the site plan draws it about ft away.
  • Shape and length. The wall follows the berm's outline: ft along the north face, two quarter-circle ends of ft radius, and a ft return at each end to the pit's north wall line, about ft in all and sq ft of face. A material that cannot curve can be built in straight facets. Creosote-treated ties are a poor choice beside food beds.
  • Timing. The wall's end arcs pass about ft from the open cut's north corners, so build the wall after the shoring is in and the cut is backfilled and compacted, then place the berm in lifts behind it.

How It Gets Built

  1. Before anything: a perc test and a test hole to about ft; call 811; stake the corners from the fence.
  2. Swales first, so water has somewhere to go while the ground is open.
  3. Dig, keeping spoil and machines at least 2 ft back from the edge. The open cut is about at the surface if sloped at 1.5:1 (OSHA's figure for the weakest soil), or narrower if the walls are shored as they are dug. Nobody works alone in an unprotected cut deeper than about 4 ft, and the walls are inspected after every storm.
  4. Posts and walls, as the book describes: set the posts, charred and bagged below grade, then hold the earth back with boards over polyethylene.
  5. Beams, rafters, and roof, then the retaining wall at the berm's foot, with its drain and gravel, and the berm placed in lifts behind it and against the north wall.
  6. Drums, beds, stair, and sump; seed the berm before winter.

The rules and the schedule are on the site plan: Idaho Code 39-4116(5) (agricultural building exemption), Idaho Code 55-2205 (811), Elmore County's exempt-work list (retaining walls under 4 feet, unless supporting surcharge), and OSHA 29 CFR 1926.651 and 1926.652 (excavations).
