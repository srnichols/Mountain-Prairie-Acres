Workshop Pole Barn

The Building in Brief

Use
RV garage, workshop, and storage loft. What the building really holds decides its permit path: see County rules
Camper
2024 Forest River Rockwood Mini Lite 2517S towable, 25′-11″ × 8′ × 10′-11″ (length × width × height, the manufacturer's listed exterior size); 5,774 lb dry, 7,615 lb GVWR (fully loaded). The first story is sized to drive it in: the door is 12 ft tall and there is 13′-2″ clear under the loft floor
Footprint
32 × 48 ft: 1,536 sq ft on the main floor and the same on the loft floor, of which about 950 sq ft has 7 ft or more of headroom (19.75 ft wide down the middle)
Bays
Sidewall posts at 8 ft: 8-8-8-8-8-8, with 19 piers and 25 trusses at 2 ft OC. The length is a whole number of 8 ft bays, so the posts, the 16 ft girts, the 4×8 sheets, and the 36 in steel panels land on a clean module
Wall framing
2×6 girts laid flat on the outside face of the posts at 24 in OC, screwed to every post: 7 rows on each wall, the lowest pressure-treated. The boards are 16 ft, two bays, so every joint falls on a post; alternate rows start with an 8 ft piece so neighboring rows never join over the same post, and 18 ft boards cut in half fill the 9 ft runs beside the east door. The sheathing goes over the girts, so the wall is about 10″ thick (wall framing sheets)
Heights
14 ft eave (the loft floor), 22 ft at the pitch break, 24.5 ft at the ridge, all above the floor. The floor is 1′-1″ above the pad, so the eave is 15′-1″ above the pad, the ridge 25′-7″, and the mid-point of the gable 20′-4″. The gambrel slopes are 49° below the break and 16° above it. The county's pole barn guide, which does not bind an engineered building, puts its usual limits at a 14 ft side wall and a 27 ft peak, and the building stays inside both, measured from the floor (County rules asks where the county takes grade)
Orientation
Long side east–west; the main door is on the east gable, facing the paths and the RV pad, so a prevailing west wind meets the closed back wall. The baselines do not settle which way the winter wind blows
Design loads
120 psf ground snow (the county's zone for Prairie, and the load it required of a neighbor's building) · 115 mph wind, Exposure C · 40+ psf loft floor · seismic category set by the engineer
Main door
14′ × 12′ insulated, wind-rated sectional overhead door with low-headroom track, centered on the east gable. At 12 ft it clears the camper's roof by 1′-1″; at 14 ft wide it leaves 3′-0″ each side of the camper for backing a trailer in (a 12 ft door would leave 2 ft). The loft floor leaves 14″ above the door for the track
Loft doors
5′ × 6′ insulated double doors on the east gable, above the main door at loft-floor level, opening onto the deck
Hoist beam
W6 steel I-beam reaching 12′-6″ out through the east gable, 2 ft past the deck's outer edge, so a load lifts from the ground clear of the deck and comes in over the rail. Two raking braces rise from the deck's outer corners to the beam's tip. A 1-ton chain fall with a 25 ft lift, for loads of 500–1,000 lb
Landing deck
14′ × 10′, free-standing on 6×6 posts to grade, 42″ rail; reached from the loft only (no outside stairs)
Walk door
3′ × 7′ insulated steel, south wall near the east end
Windows
3 in the north wall and 3 in the south wall, 4′ × 3′ insulated sliders with the sill 5′ above the floor, centered in their bays and clear of the stair and the walk door; one 3′ × 4′ single-hung in the west gable at the loft. About $1,900 to $3,500 in materials with their flashing and trim
Loft access
L-shaped stairs of 24 risers of 7 in: the first flight (11 risers) along the south wall to a landing at the southwest corner 6′-5″ up, then the second flight (13 risers) up the west wall through a 4′ × 11′ opening in the loft floor
Heat and power
Wood stove and a mini-split heat pump (cooling too) · 200 A sub-panel; where it is fed from is still open
Colors

Built by the owner with family, with pre-fab stamped trusses, in phases if needed. The goal is weather-tight before snow flies

Where It Sits

The workshop goes on the graded pad north of the pump house and the containers, with its east door lined up with the road-mix path and the RV path. Its footprint is in data/site.json at 32 × 48 ft, centered on the pad.

Notes on the pad and the foundation

  • Fill and compaction. Ask the engineer whether the fill was placed in compacted lifts. If that is not documented, plan to compact and test it, or to deepen the gravel base, before the slab goes on it.
  • The pier holes go through the fill. The 60 in is measured from the pad surface, so the north row's holes pass through about 2 ft of fill and end about 3 ft into native soil, and the south row's end about 4 to 4½ ft into it. Each pier pad should bear on undisturbed native soil, or on fill the engineer accepts. Detail 3 and Section A draw this.
  • The north edge. The fill is thickest along the north edge, where the pad reaches only 5 ft beyond the wall (4 ft past each end). Check that the edge is firm and not slumping, and keep the north wall's piers and the slab edge well inside it.
  • The pad surface. The lidar predates the pad, so shoot the finished surface with a level and record it here. The slab, foam, and gravel stack up about 13 in above it, so the floor sits about 1′-1″ above the pad.

County Rules and What They Mean Here

From Elmore County's published Zoning and Development Ordinance (Title 7, chapters 1 and 2, adopted May 2018, amended 2023 and 2024), the building regulations (Title 9), the county's Notice of Proposed New Agricultural Building, and its Pole Barn/Post and Beam Standards (approved July 19, 2021), read in October 2026 at elmorecounty.org. The ordinance is being amended (case ZOA-2026-01, to match the updated Comprehensive Plan; the staff report for the October 22, 2026 hearing recommends continuing the review to November 18, 2026). The draft keeps the height table and the accessory- and agricultural-structure standards cited below, but it reorganizes chapter 2, so section numbers may change. This is a planning summary, not legal advice and not the county's answer: read the current text and get each answer in writing.

Which permit path is this building on?

The county sorts a building by what it is used for, not by its name.

  • Accessory building: the design as drawn. An RV garage, a workshop, and a storage loft. The house comes first, and the height, size, and matching rules above apply. It needs a building permit, plan review, and inspections. At 120 psf the county's pole barn guide, which lets a simple barn skip the engineer, does not apply (see the table), so the plans and the trusses need an Idaho engineer's stamp.
  • Agricultural building. A barn for orchard equipment, a tractor, bins, and harvest storage. It is exempt from the building code, but not from zoning: it needs zoning approval before construction and the county's agricultural exemption in place of a building permit, and the district's setbacks and height still apply. It must stand on a farm (5 acres or more in agricultural use, tied to the assessor's tax categories), so ask whether the parcel qualifies. The owner signs a notarized statement, recorded with the county, that the building is used solely for agriculture.

The two cannot be mixed: the definition says solely. With the RV inside, the building is on the first path, and a building later put to another use can be required to obtain every permit and prove it meets the code. If the plan changes to a farm barn, the package should be redrawn for that use (a different name, no camper clearances), and the application should describe what the building will hold.

Before Any Site Work

  1. Call the Elmore County Land Use and Building Department (208-587-2130, extension 6500): confirm the wind speed, frost depth, and exposure for this parcel, and which rules apply at about 4,750 ft. The ground snow load is settled at 120 psf: the county required a neighbor's building to be designed for it.
  2. Settle the permit path with the county, in writing (see County rules): the parcel's zoning district (some of the rules apply only in residential districts); whether this is an accessory building or an agricultural building, which follows from what it will really hold; how the height is measured, from what grade (for the pole barn guide's 14 ft side wall and 27 ft peak as well as the 24 ft accessory limit), and how a gambrel is averaged; the front-yard size cap; and whether the house must be permitted first.
  3. Get stamped truss drawings for 120 psf ground snow from a pre-fab truss company: send it the truss package, which has the collar tie, the loft stair opening, the loft doors, and the hoist-beam loads on it.
  4. Get stamped foundation and structural plans from an Idaho-licensed engineer for 120 psf ground snow and 115 mph wind, including what to do about the pad's fill.
  5. Pull the building permit.
  6. Call 811 before any digging. The buried 1.5 in water line is about 11 ft south of the south wall and the west-fence line about 16 ft west.
  7. Settle the power source and run the sub-feed conduit before backfill.
  8. Shoot the pad's elevation with a level and get the fill checked before the footings.

Open Questions

  1. What will the building really hold? That decides the permit path (see County rules). An RV garage and a workshop make it an accessory building, which is what these drawings show. A barn for orchard equipment and harvest could be an agricultural building. It cannot be both.
  2. Where does the power come from? The pump house with its solar inverters and batteries, or the house? A 200 A panel is a panel rating, not a supply: check that the source can run the welder (50 A), the compressor (30 A), and the mini-split together.
  3. How heavy a load will the hoist lift? The beam is sized for 1,000 lb at its tip, with the two braces carrying that load down into the deck. A heavier load, such as an engine or a full pallet, changes the beam, the braces, and the deck's piers, so decide what it will lift before the engineer sizes them.
  4. How far does the slab slope? The slab slopes toward the east door. At ¼ in per ft over 48 ft that is 12 in, which is a lot. Slope only the 12 to 16 ft nearest the door, or use ⅛ in per ft; decide before the forms are set.
  5. Stove, condenser, and panel. The wood stove's spot is not chosen. The mini-split condenser goes on the west or the south wall.
  6. How will the inside walls be insulated and finished? The girts are on the outside of the posts, so between the posts there is nothing to fasten insulation or an interior finish to. A conditioned shop needs horizontal strapping across the posts (2×4 laid flat at 24 in OC, about 1,120 ft in all) or a framed inner wall, with batts in the 7¼ in bays. The materials estimate prices the batts and the finish sheets but not the strapping, so settle this before the second phase is priced.
  7. The winter wind. The door is on the east end on the idea that a prevailing west wind hits the closed back wall. If the winter wind comes from the east instead, the door and the deck take it and the drifts pile up there. The weather station's first year should settle it.
  8. Camper height. The drawings use the manufacturer's listed height, 10′-11″. That leaves 1′-1″ under the 12 ft door head and 2′-3″ under the loft floor. Measure it as it will be parked, hitched, and check that the figure takes in the roof air conditioner and anything else up there. A taller door needs a taller wall and longer posts, and a wall over the pole barn guide's usual 14 ft is the engineer's call, since this building is engineered anyway.
  9. How does the county apply the 14 ft wall and the 27 ft peak to an engineered building? The pole barn guide states them for a barn without an engineer, and lets the Building Official consider a stamped alternate design. It does not say where it measures them from. From the floor the wall is 14′-0″; from the pad surface it is 15′-1″. The roof peak, with about 9 in of purlins, deck, steel, and ridge cap on the trusses, is about 26′-4″ above the pad, 8 in under 27 ft. Measured from the original ground, which is about 1.3 ft lower under the east wall, it would be about 8 in over.
  10. Is the upper roof slope steep enough? It is 3.3 in 12 (16°), kept low so the roof peak stays under the county's usual 27 ft. Ask the steel supplier for the minimum slope of its exposed-fastener panel (many ask for at least 3 in 12, or sealant at the laps below that), and ask the engineer how much snow drifts at the pitch break at 120 psf on a slope this flat, and whether a steeper slope is worth a taller peak.
  11. How will the roof be insulated? The truss package keeps the loft's room open between the pitch-break lines, crossing it only with the collar ties (8′-0″ up, on T1 to T20). A ceiling on those ties would be flat across only the middle 13′-6″ of the loft, so blown-in insulation on a flat ceiling cannot cover the roof. The insulation has to follow the roof instead: spray foam against the deck, or batts between the top chords with a vent channel above them, with the 12 in heel giving it depth at the eaves. Decide the method with the engineer and the truss plant, and price it: the materials estimate carries blown-in cellulose on a flat ceiling as a placeholder.

How to Use This Package

  1. Verify the design loads and the county rules (see County rules) with Elmore County before any orders go out.
  2. Use the specs when calling the steel vendor or the concrete supplier; read straight off the page. Send the truss package (or its PDF) to the truss plant: it holds the shape, the open room and its collar tie, the loads, and the special trusses.
  3. Use the plans and elevations for layout on site and with the permit; the framing in perspective shows how the posts, girts, trusses, and purlins fit together, and the wall framing sheets show every girt board.
  4. Use the sections and details when framing, sheathing, and siding; the numbered and lettered markers match the legends.
  5. Use the materials list as the shopping and check-off list. Firm prices replace the estimates as quotes come in.
