Finch-Style Pit Greenhouse: 17 × 96 ft

A closed-loop earth-tube house in the published Greenhouse in the Snow size, drawn so it can be priced against the unheated 20 × 36 ft pit · not sited, not ordered, and not engineered · a draft for review

This is an independent reconstruction, not a copy of the Greenhouse in the Snow build manual, joint plates, or catalog drawings. The published sizes used here are the 17 ft width, the 96 ft length, the 6 ft bays, a floor about 4 ft down, 4 in closed-loop tubes about 8 ft down, and 8 mm twin-wall sheets 14 ft by 71.25 in. Where they have not published a number, the page says it is our choice. The 20 × 36 ft pit remains the planned pit. This house is the other option. It is not on the site master grid.

Read this before treating it as a build set. The roof has not been checked against the 120 lb/sq ft ground snow load used on this place. The rafter size is deliberately not given. Their original wood greenhouse in Alliance collapsed under 8 ft of snow in 2022. About 10 ft of Simonton soil sits on basalt here, so an 8 ft tube trench has about 2 ft of soil under it. Probe the rock before believing the tube field.

The Design in Brief

  • Room: , long axis east to west, glass facing south. Sixteen bays of 6 ft. The west bay is an airlock. The east bay is a work bay with the second exit. The middle ft is beds and a walk.
  • Floor: one level, ft below grade. Raised beds sit 2 ft above that floor. Their later published figure is 1.2 m (about 4 ft). An older kit article said 5 ft. Four feet is used here so the post bottoms, 2 ft under the floor, stay clear of the rock.
  • Roof: our choice, because they have not published the angle. A south slope, one 14 ft sheet long, so there is no mid-slope joint. That slope covers ft of the 17 ft width. The north ft is an insulated cap, pitched 2:12 to the north so it is not a flat pond. The south eave is ft above grade. The ridge is ft above grade. The north wall is ft from the floor to its top, of which ft stands above grade.
  • Glazing: sheets of 8 mm twin-wall, 14 ft by 71.25 in, one per bay, UV face out, flutes running down the slope. Ends taped: solid tape at the high end, vented tape at the low end, so the flutes drain and do not fill with algae. A typical rating for this sheet is about R-1.7. The tunnels' four-layer panel is R-2.2 and is a better night blanket, but it is not the sheet they publish for this house.
  • Tubes: closed loops of solid 4 in corrugated HDPE, scaled from their published 13 loops on a 78 ft house (one loop per 6 ft bay). Each loop is 250 ft, with about 200 ft buried 8 ft down and the rest in the risers. The field is north of the house: . Not perforated drain tile. Joints sealed so the blowers cannot suck soil gas.
  • Heat: the tubes run first. A propane unit heater is the backup, because on this lot the ground at depth is near , not the 52°F they quote for Alliance. The heater is sized to hold the room alone on a hard night, so a dead blower does not freeze it.

What It Can Hold Here

Alliance sits near 42.1°N and about 3,900 ft. This lot is 43.47°N and about 4,750 ft. Their ground figure of 52°F does not travel. The Prairie SNOTEL monthly averages, high and low together, mean . Soil near 8 to 10 ft is usually close to that. It has not been measured here.

Under the assumptions in the heat table, the tubes can hold inside only while the night stays above about . A normal January night here averages . The tubes do not carry that night. A night insulation blanket on the glass moves the balance only to about . December 21 is a hour day, under the 10 hours they say most plants need. Lights can stretch the day. They cannot make the ground warmer.

  • A fair job for this house: a bright winter workroom, and cool greens, with the propane heater carrying the nights below freezing outside. The tubes cut the hours the heater runs. They are not the heater.
  • Not this house: citrus or other plants that want nights near 50°F, unless the heater and the lights run as a matter of course. Their own later notes recommend a backup heater. Finch used a small propane forced-air heater a few times in 23 years in Alliance. That climate is not this one, and that count is not a design load.
  • Against the planned pit: the 20 × 36 ft pit is the start house and the late house, and it does not need an 8 ft trench in 10 ft of soil. This 96 ft house is the option only if winter work, and a real heater, are worth the tube field.

The Tube Field

  • Pipe: ft of solid 4 in corrugated HDPE ( ft of it in the ground). Smooth-wall pipe moves the same air with less friction if it can be had; the published material is corrugated, so that is the count.
  • Slope and water: pitch each buried leg about 1% so condensate runs to a low point, and give that point a cleanout and a drain to its own dry well. Do not dump tube water into the beds. Standing water in a closed loop is how these pipes go sour.
  • How to dig it: plan on type C soil until a competent person says otherwise, the same rule as the site plan. An 8 ft open cut at 1½:1 is about 26 ft wide at the top. Sixteen of those are one hole, not sixteen ditches. The practical dig is a trench shield, about 2 ft wide in the shield, or the air tubes are not dug. Shielded volume is about cu yd in the ground, and it all goes back as backfill except the pipe.
  • Rock: stop if the probe finds basalt above 9 ft anywhere on the field. Switch to the liquid loop under the floor, described below, or drop this option.
  • Blowers: their catalog sells a ½ hp, 115 V squirrel-cage blower per tube circuit, in a galvanized box, on a thermostat that spans 30 to 110°F. One per loop is 16 motors. That is a subpanel, not a spare breaker. A manifold with fewer blowers is simpler and is not what they publish; either way an electrician sizes the circuits. The heat table assumes about cfm through all 16 loops together, which is 400 ft/min in a 4 in pipe. Faster air picks up less heat per pass.
  • Controls: tube thermostat at 40°F, so the loops lead. Heater thermostat at 34°F, so propane fires only when the loops cannot hold the room. A winter/summer switch, which they sell, keeps the same fans from heating the house in July. Horizontal airflow fans, one every 20 to 25 ft, stop the ridge from stratifying. End vents stay shut in winter.

If the rock says no

Their short kits, and any long kit that sits in a high water table, use a liquid-to-air loop instead: nontoxic antifreeze in smaller trenches, through a coil and a fan, all inside the footprint. They do not publish the trench layout. For this lot the owner-build version is a closed propylene-glycol loop in trenches under the floor, bottom about 6 ft below grade (2 ft under the floor, about 4 ft above the rock if the rock is at 10 ft), headers in the north strip, a circulator, and a fan-coil. The coil size is not designed on this page. Their June 2026 catalog prices that hardware, shipped, at $ for a house 90 to 126 ft long. Buying that unit is a price check, not a requirement.

Build Sequence

Stake nothing until the rock probe and the snow-load engineer have answered. The sequence below is the order of the work, not a permit.

  1. Probe. Auger or dig test holes on the tube-field centerline and at both ends of the house, to 9 ft or to rock, whichever comes first. Call 811 before any of it. If rock is above 9 ft, do not lay out the air tubes.
  2. Stake. A 96 × 17 ft rectangle, long axis east to west, within a few degrees of true south. The glass faces south. Leave 100 ft clear to the north if the air tubes stay in the plan, plus room for the spoil. The east stair well adds about 4 × 8 ft outside the east wall.
  3. Strip and dig the pit to 4 ft below grade inside the walls. Set the subgrade so a 4 in collector in the gravel under the walk falls about 1% to a sump at the east end of the walk. Keep the brick walk itself nearly level. An open cut follows the type C slopes on the site plan unless it is shielded.
  4. Posts and shoring. Pressure-treated 6×6 posts on the perimeter, 6 ft on center along the long walls, bottoms 6 ft below grade (2 ft under the floor). Shore the cut with 2× boards and 6 mil polyethylene, the same method as the Oehler pit. Corners are shared. End walls get posts at 6 ft and 12 ft off the north wall; the last bay on a 17 ft end is 5 ft.
  5. Drain and insulation at the perimeter. A 4 in perforated drain at the footing, to daylight or a dry well, not into the tube dry well. Two inches of extruded polystyrene (about R-10) on the outside of the shoring, from the floor to grade, and a 24 in horizontal wing at the bottom. This is our thickness. They sell perimeter insulation and do not publish the inches.
  6. Floor. Four inches of compacted gravel, then the collector and the sump, then pavers or brick when the shell is closed. No slab is required. Slope only the gravel.
  7. Walls above grade. A treated plate on the shoring. North wall and end walls: 2×6 at 16 in, R-21 batts, a taped warm-side vapor layer (6 mil poly or foil-faced foam) so greenhouse moisture does not soak the batts, then metal siding outside. The south knee is the same build, only ft above grade. The north wall from grade to its top is about ft. A 4 ft retaining wall that carries this building is not clearly inside Elmore County's under-4-ft exemption, because the wall holds a surcharge. Read the county list before calling it exempt. The agricultural-building note on the site plan is the place to start, not a ruling.
  8. North cap and rafters. Frame the cap at 2:12, insulated to about R-30, metal roofing, overhang enough to drip clear of the wall. The south rafters carry the 45° glass. Do not pick them from a 30 or 40 lb snow table. The number used on this place is 120 lb/sq ft on the ground. A steep roof sheds snow better than the 27° pit roof, and it can also dump a slab of it in front of the south eave. An engineer sizes the rafters, the glazing bars, and the connections before any of that lumber is bought. Eight-millimeter twin-wall does not span 6 ft under that load by itself.
  9. Glaze. Sixteen sheets, UV face out, flutes downhill, high end taped solid, low end taped vented, seated in gasketed bars with rubber-backed washers. Do not cinch the washers flat. Leave the sheet room to move. A wind clip at each bar is worth adding on this site; they sell one for high-wind places.
  10. Doors and the second way out. West door at grade, into the airlock, then seven steps down to the floor (48 in of drop, in risers, 10 in treads, 5 ft of run). The stair runs across the width, not along the 6 ft bay, because a landing plus a 5 ft run will not fit in 6 ft end to end. Inner door at floor level. East door at floor level, opening into an exterior stair well with its own drain. A ladder is not the second exit.
  11. Tubes or liquid loop, then the blowers, the horizontal fans, and the end vents. One vent high in each end wall, thermostat-opened, pan sloped so it does not hold water. Summer cooling is a fan at one end and, if the house overheats, an evaporative pad at the other. Their catalog pairs those two and says the wall fans then need an adapter because they cannot sit in the wall. Winter vents stay shut.
  12. Heater. A vented propane unit heater of about BTU/hr, thermostat at 34°F, hung where the flame and the exhaust cannot touch poly or a bed. That size covers the record-night load in the table, not just a mild night. Set it after the tube thermostat, not instead of a CO alarm.
  13. Close the shell before a hard night. Their original house, once the roof failed, was below zero inside within a day or two. This design stores hours, not a week.

Heat Balance

This is a steady-state check, not a simulation. It is here so the propane heater is not a surprise. Conduction uses the areas below. Infiltration is air changes an hour, which is a tight house; a leaky door doubles that line. The tubes are credited only for air that enters at and leaves partway to the soil, at the effectiveness named in the table. Below-grade walls are left out of the loss: if the perimeter foam is in place, that soil is warmer than the room we are trying to hold, so it is a small gain, not a night loss. Items to Settle

Where it would sit

  • Not in E14 and F14. That pad is the 20 × 36 ft pit. This house is 96 ft long, and the air-tube field wants about 100 ft to the north of that.
  • South glass must see December sun. Anything tall to the south, including a future berm, shades the collector the design depends on.
  • The east stair well and the tube-drain dry well need a place that is not the uphill swale.

Ground

  • Rock depth along the whole tube run, not one hole. Basalt at 10 ft is the owner's report and the soil survey's order of magnitude, not a trench log.
  • Water table unknown. Their liquid loop is the published answer for a high table. A floor 4 ft down cannot sit in the water either way.
  • A soil temperature at 8 ft, measured, would replace the estimate.

Structure and power

  • Rafters, glazing bars, and the north cap, checked at 120 lb/sq ft ground snow. No member size on this page is approved for construction.
  • Sixteen ½ hp motors, the vent fans, lights, and the heater need a subpanel. That load is not designed here.
  • Whether to buy their galvanized frame instead of building the wood walls above grade. The kit does not remove the excavation, the retaining, the tubes, or the engineer.

What "above freezing" costs

  • The heater, the CO alarm, and the propane are part of the design, not extras.
  • A night blanket is the cheapest way to cut the glazing loss. It is still not a substitute for the heater in January.
  • If the goal is only starts and a late tomato, build the 20 × 36 ft pit and stop.
