Workshop Pole Barn: Materials and Cost Estimate

Source codes: HD Home Depot · LO Lowe's · SP specialty supplier · Q vendor quote required. Every quantity is computed from the building's dimensions in docs/workshop_model.js, and every total is the quantity times the unit price, summed. Prices are 2026 ballpark ranges, not quotes.

This is a planning estimate, not a fixed-price bid

Prices are 2026 ballpark ranges for the Treasure Valley and Boise area. Lumber moves with commodity prices; steel and trusses must be quoted by your supplier with current numbers. The Midpoint column is halfway between each line's low and high, so it is a planning number, not a prediction.

Items marked Q must be quoted: the pre-fabricated trusses, the steel package, the laminated columns, and the wind-rated overhead door together are roughly 40–50% of the materials cost, and prices vary a lot by supplier. The truss price in particular is a placeholder until quotes arrive.

Summary by Phase

Not in the materials total

  • Labor. Owner-build assumes you provide it; a hired contractor adds 30–50%.
  • Permits, plan review, and engineering, pier holes and dirt work, the power feeder trench and connection (the specs call for no water or septic), equipment rental, and sales tax: these are in the budget table at the end of this page.
  • Solar, generator, and batteries are an owner-future item and are not in either table.

Shopping Strategy and Sourcing

From Home Depot or Lowe's

  • Dimensional lumber (2×4, 2×6, 2×8, 2×10): open accounts at both, and check prices weekly
  • OSB sheathing (½″ and ⅝″): get the Pro-desk price and ask about bunk pricing
  • PT lumber (1×4 furring, 2×10 splash board, 6×6 deck posts)
  • Tyvek and Tyvek tape (Tyvek brand, not generic)
  • Ice and water shield and synthetic underlayment
  • Simpson connectors (hangers, hurricane ties, structural screws)
  • Rigid foam (XPS) for under the slab
  • Electrical: panel, breakers, wire, conduit, devices, LED fixtures
  • The 3070 walk door (in stock or a one-week order)
  • Concrete extras (rebar, Sonotube, sealer), caulk, adhesive, fasteners, and hardware

From a specialty or regional supplier

  • Trusses: Western Truss (Caldwell), BMC, Builders FirstSource. 4–8 week lead time. PE stamp.
  • Laminated columns: Perma-Column, Sturdi-Wall (Mid-States Building), Hansen Buildings, or site-build.
  • Steel package: Idaho Steel Products (Caldwell), Boise Steel Roofing, Metal Sales (Twin Falls). Get an itemized quote with the full trim package.
  • Wet-set brackets: usually ship with the laminated column order.
  • 14×14 overhead door: a local overhead-door dealer, with an installed quote.
  • Insulated chimney: a fireplace specialty shop (Idaho Hearth).
  • Ready-mix concrete: the closest plant to Prairie (CalPortland Boise, Idaho Concrete in Mountain Home).
  • Snow guards: direct from the manufacturer (SnowTrapper, S-5!).
  • Crushed gravel: a local pit, much cheaper than bagged.

Quote-required items: do these first

  1. Trusses (engineered and stamped)
  2. The laminated column package and brackets
  3. The steel roof, wall, and trim package
  4. The 14×14 overhead door (installed)
  5. Ready-mix concrete (per-yard price and short-load fees)

These five items determine about half of the project cost. Get them quoted before you swing a hammer.

Cost-cut levers (if the budget is tight)

  • Site-build the posts instead of buying them laminated ($1,500 saved)
  • Skip the interior finish for now ($1,800 saved)
  • Skip the insulation and install it when needed ($3,000 saved)
  • Skip the mini-split and run the wood stove only ($2,500 saved)
  • A 3070 walk door without windows, or lower grade ($300 saved)
  • Fewer windows: the 7 windows with their flashing and trim are about $1,900 to $3,500 together
  • A standard (non-insulated) wind-rated overhead door ($800 saved, but you will regret it in winter)
  • A single-color steel package ($300 saved)
  • PT plywood instead of 5/4 PT decking ($200 saved)
  • Defer the landing deck to a later phase ($1,800 saved)

Do NOT cut these

  • Engineered trusses with a PE stamp. 120 psf snow is non-negotiable.
  • Wet-set brackets. Direct-burial posts rot in 15–25 years.
  • Rainscreen furring. The whole reason for OSB behind the steel is wasted without it.
  • Tyvek, not Typar or a cheap generic. Commercial-grade WRB lasts 50 years.
  • Ice and water shield on the full lower slope. Failure here puts water in the loft.
  • The 2×10 PT splash board. Do not substitute a 2×8 or skip it.
  • 4″ rigid foam under the slab (or at least 2″ XPS). Without it the slab will sweat.
  • A wind-rated overhead door. Very few off-the-shelf doors meet 115 mph.

Contingency and Total Project Budget

Reality check: plan on the Midpoint column (about $139,900) as your real budget, and on the High column (about $179,500) as the number that makes you stop and reassess. Material costs are volatile: lumber alone can swing 30% in one quarter. Locking in the steel and truss prices early protects about half of the spend.

Length and Cost

Each foot of length

  • Each foot adds about $970 of materials at the midpoint, about $1,200 all in. The trusses, the steel, the concrete, the foam, the wrap, and the ice-and-water shield grow with the length; the doors, the stair, the deck, and the hoist do not, so a longer building costs less per square foot. Materials come to about $67 per sq ft of floor.
  • The estimate takes no credit for less waste. The length is a whole number of 8 ft bays, so the posts, the 16 ft girts, the 4×8 sheets, and the 36 in steel panels land on a clean module and need little trimming. Every quantity here scales with the length, so the real cost is probably a few hundred dollars lower. Ask the steel and truss suppliers to quote the package at the length you build.
