The Plan in Brief

PRISM puts Prairie at about 22 in of precipitation a year, about double the ~11 in often quoted for Elmore County, which reflects the low desert around Mountain Home. NRCS lists 12–16 in for your soil's setting, so your lot may get less; a rain gauge will settle it. Either way it's more than Lancaster gets in Tucson. The catch is timing: about two-thirds falls from November through March, and about half of the year's total arrives as snow while the trees are dormant. July through September brings only about an inch, just when a mature orchard's demand peaks. The plan:

  • Start at the top. The homestead roofs and gravel pad sit at the high point just south of the orchard. Catch their water and spread it into the top rows.
  • Catch it where it lands. A basin at every tree soaks snowmelt and spring rain deep, filling the soil "tank" before summer.
  • Pass the overflow downhill. Each basin spills to the next tree, and the chain ends in a sponge basin at the low northwest corner by the road.
  • Let the drip fill the July–September gap. Harvested water won't replace the drip, but it can delay the first run, shorten the season, and soften dry spells.

      Lancaster's Eight Principles, Applied Here

      #Principle (Lancaster's wording)On your site
      1Begin with long and thoughtful observation.Walk the block during snowmelt and after summer storms. Flag where water runs and ponds and where snow and frost linger, and use the log on the last page.
      2Start at the top (highpoint) of your watershed and work your way down.Your high point is the homestead pad: its roofs and gravel come first, then the driveway and the top rows. The northwest corner by the road comes last.
      3Start small and simple.One crescent per tree at planting. Add the spreader, driveway basins, lane, and corner basin after a season of watching.
      4Slow, spread, and infiltrate the flow of water.Level basin floors and level berm crests; many small basins instead of one ditch; mulch everywhere water lands.
      5Always plan an overflow route, and manage that overflow as a resource.Every basin spills to the next tree downhill, and the chain ends in the northwest sponge basin, not the road.
      6Maximize living and organic groundcover.Wood chips in the basins, perennial cover in the alleys, clippings blown onto the rows.
      7Maximize beneficial relationships and efficiency by "stacking functions."The windbreak is also a snow fence, a nut crop, and a mulch source; the berry guilds sit in the drift zone; elderberry cuttings plant the sponge basin; roof tanks double as gravity-fed drip.
      8Continually reassess your system: the "feedback loop."Each fall, widen basins toward the drip line and fix spots where water broke through or stood too long.

      Crescent Berm 'n Basin at Each Tree

      Build it as you plant

      1. Plant on a low mound, 3–4 in high and wider than the root ball, so the root flare ends up 1–2 in above the future spillway level after the soil settles.
      2. Find the downhill direction at the tree with a level. Expect northwest in R1–R5 and north-northwest farther north (the site plan shows each tree), but local bumps rule.
      3. Mark an arc on the downhill side, 3–4 ft from the trunk, with its ends running along the contour and curving slightly uphill.
      4. Shape a shallow, level-floored saucer between the mound and the arc, digging no deeper than about 2 in so the floor stays in the loam above the stickier clay-loam subsoil. Get the rest of the depth from the berm: pile soil on the arc (scrape extra from the alley side, never from the basin floor), tamp it, and level its crest.
      5. Cut a spillway notch in the middle of the berm so water can stand 3–4 in deep at most (3 in or less for stone fruit), with the crest 2–3 in higher. You don't need rock: line the notch with a strip of sod from the alley, or a paver or board set flush, so overflow can't cut it. Aim it at the next tree downhill, usually the northwest diagonal about 22 ft away (the site plan shows which).
      6. Mulch the basin with 2–3 in of wood chips (about 2 in for stone fruit), keeping the chip surface at least 1 in below the spillway and a 6–12 in bare ring around the trunk, and add a hardware-cloth guard.
      7. Set the drip emitters inside the basin under the outer canopy, and move them outward as the tree grows.
      8. Test with a hose or the first storm. Water should spread along the berm, not pour out one end, and soak in within about a day.
      9. Each fall, extend the arc outward toward the drip line.

      Find the contour

      An A-frame level, a water level (clear tubing filled with water), or a laser level all work; Volume 2, Appendix 1 covers all three. With an A-frame, mark where the plumb line crosses the crossbar, flip the frame end for end on the same two spots, and mark again; halfway between the marks is level. Then walk the frame across the slope, swinging one leg until the line hits the level mark, and flag each foot. The flags trace a contour. On your ground, expect contours to run east-northeast to northeast, crossing the tree rows at about 17–56°.

      Test how fast your soil drinks

      Dig a hole about 12 in deep and 8 in wide near the south end, the middle, and the northwest part of the block; its sides also show where the stickier clay loam starts. Fill it, let it drain, refill it, and measure how far the water drops in one hour. NRCS rates the clay-loam subsoil at about 0.4 in per hour (0.2–0.6), so 4 in of water should be gone in about 10 hours (7–20). For a basin to empty within about a day, keep its water depth no more than 24 times your hourly drop. Where water still stands after a day, send extra water only to wet-tolerant plants.

      Cold air follows the water. On frosty spring nights cold air drains the same way water does, toward the northwest corner by the road. With the block at the south end of the strip, that open corner, about 300 ft past R15, is the frost pocket, not the orchard. Within the block the stone fruit (R9–R12) is on the lower half but on its steepest band, so cold air keeps moving, and the apricots (R11–R12, C1–C3) are the lowest planted trees. Stone fruit blooms first, and UC IPM notes that bacterial canker, already flagged on many of these trees, is worse in low spots and after spring freezes; drought stress and irrigation that wets the trunk also set trees up for it. So these trees need water that soaks through, never water that stands, plus steady drip in summer. Don't plant a solid hedge across the block's north end, where it would dam cold air against them. NRCS lists only 60–100 frost-free days for this soil's setting, so that cold-air drainage matters.
