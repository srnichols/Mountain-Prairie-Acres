Gothic Tunnel: 27 × 76 ft

An unheated, season-extending polycarbonate tunnel in cells A13 to C13, its west end beside the windbreak alley, 20 ft south of the orchard · two bays of CatchSunlight's standard 27 × 38 ft kit, end to end · planned for 2028 or 2029 · a draft for review

Specifications are paraphrased from CatchSunlight's high-tunnel page and its drawings (read October 2026); the vendor's pages and drawings are not reproduced here. The hoop curve and the door and vent sizes in the figures are readings of the vendor's drawings, so treat them as approximate. Prices and specifications change: confirm everything with the vendor before ordering. For where the tunnel sits and how the water, shade, and soil work around it, see the site plan; for the pit greenhouse south of it, the pit page.

The Tunnel in Brief

  • What it is: a pointed-arch ("gothic") high tunnel on 48 mm galvanized steel hoops with a tube-truss roof support, covered in 6 mm four-layer polycarbonate (the vendor gives it an insulating value of R-2.2). The vendor is an Arco, Idaho company, and its site says the frames are designed by its founder, a licensed professional engineer.
  • Standard size: 27 ft wide by 38 ft long (about 1,000 sq ft), sold as a do-it-yourself materials kit at $10,500 as published. The page says there is "no limit on length," that hoop spacing can open up from 45 to 80 in to stretch the length, and to call for custom sizes and prices.
  • Here: two standard bays end to end make ( sq ft), long axis east to west at , with a wide door and a peak vent in each end wall (see End Walls). Two standard kits would cost about in materials at the published price: a quote for the real length is still needed.
  • Unheated: the tunnel extends the season; it is not a winter-production greenhouse with a heater. That keeps it inside the kind of structure NRCS's high-tunnel program covers (to be confirmed; see the site plan).

End Walls: the Wide Door and the Vents

  • Two layouts. The vendor draws two end walls, both 12 ft at the peak. The standard one has an 80 in door in the bay beside the center line and a vent at the peak; the other is "framed with a wider center door opening and a vent above." Figure 3 redraws both. Only people and carts go inside the tunnel, no tractor. This draft puts the wide door at both ends, so a cart can run straight through the 76 ft, door to door; either end could keep the standard door instead.
  • The wide door has a frame about , against for the standard door: roughly twice the width and a few inches more height. The vendor gives no size for it, only the drawing, and the clear opening will be smaller than the frame. The test is the cart: measure it, wheels and load, against the standard door's clear opening before deciding that a wide door is needed.
  • Wide doors at both ends line the cart lane up with a door at each end, so a cart can enter at one end and leave by the other without backing out, and a breeze can run the whole length with both open. With a standard door, a cart enters beside the center line and turns into the lane from the end aisle. A second wide door is an added cost; the lane itself costs sq ft of bed against the most-beds layout (see Bed Layout). If the cart fits the standard door's clear opening, the wide door is a convenience to price, not a need. If only one end gets it, the west end faces the alley and the hydrant and the east end faces the yard (see Doors and access below).
  • The vents are one peak vent in each end wall, each, set up, out of reach from the floor. The vendor's parts list for the standard kit has 10 hinges, 8 latches, and 4 handles "for vents and doors," which matches one door and one vent in each end wall. So the "few vents" are these two unless more are ordered. Ask how they open and are held (a pole, a cord, an automatic opener) and whether more vent bays can be framed into the end wall.
  • Ventilation check. With both doors and both vents open, the end openings total about sq ft, percent of the sq ft floor. UMass Extension, quoting the ASABE, gives 15 to 20 percent of the floor for the ridge vents and again for the side vents of a greenhouse that has them ( to sq ft each here), and it says growers find roll-up sides work well in hoophouses. That is a yardstick for conventional greenhouses, not a rule for this kit, but it suggests a 76 ft run will lean on the wind through its two ends unless sides or a ridge can open. Ask what the vendor advises for 76 ft.

  • How it compares. Purdue's handbook works out plantable area for a 96 × 30 ft tunnel with 3 ft end alleys: 62 to 70 percent, the best (70.3) with 3.75 ft beds and 1 ft alleys. Layout A reaches percent here with 18 in rows, the low end of UGA's ideal 18 to 24 in; B reaches percent. If the beds are flat, not framed, A can become B in a season when a cart needs to run the length: the two center beds and the row between them make a strip ft wide, enough for the lane and two ft beds.
  • Lane width. The lane costs sq ft of bed for each foot of width (a strip a foot wide along the beds' full length). The 4 ft in layout B is UGA's allowance for carts, wheelbarrows, and wheelchairs; it is not a measurement of the cart that will be used here. Measure that cart with a person walking beside it, and size the lane to it.
  • Where crops go. The roof is low along the walls: ft high 1 ft in from the wall, 6 ft high ft in, and 9 ft high ft in. The 2 ft edge beds suit low crops, and Purdue says the edges of a tunnel are colder and, when the sides or ends are open, windier, so they are the place for hardy crops like spinach and kale. The inner beds have 7 ft or more overhead: Purdue puts tall crops on the north side so they do not shade shorter ones, or up the middle where the roof is highest. The vendor's truss ties the hoops together 7 ft up (Figure 1), so tall crops under it are probably held near that height unless their supports pass between the hoops. Ask the vendor, and whether crop strings can hang from the hoops or the chord.
  • Getting around. Every walking row opens into an end aisle, so anyone coming through a door can reach any row, and an end aisle touches the end of every bed. An 18 in row takes feet, not a cart: UGA allows 4 ft between beds for carts and wheelbarrows, which is layout B's lane. In B a cart runs the ft down the center line and touches the two beds beside it; the other four are a short carry across 18 in rows, or can be served from a cart parked in an end aisle. Purdue also favors long beds because an alley near the center of the end wall makes it easy to move harvest bins and larger equipment the length of the structure; here that equipment is a cart.
  • Water. UGA notes that drip irrigation is much easier to install when beds are in an orderly pattern, preferably straight. A header across the west end aisle, the end nearest the hydrant ( ft from the west door), could feed the drip tape down the beds: linear bed feet, before counting how many lines each bed takes.
  • To decide. Layout A or B; how wide the cart is (measure it, with its load) and so how wide the lane needs to be, and whether it fits the standard door; flat or framed beds; what goes in the 2 ft edge beds; and what to do with the two end aisles ( sq ft, percent of the floor). Purdue notes that tunnel corners are often under-used and suggests a perennial herb or plants for beneficial insects there.

Questions to Settle

For CatchSunlight

  • The design ground snow load and wind speed for a 76 ft run. The county's maps say lb/sq ft (the load the county required of a neighbor's building) and mph; what changes at 76 ft (hoop spacing, extra trusses, bracing)?
  • Post depth and footings against a 30 in frost depth, and how the posts step on ground that rises about 2 ft along the length.
  • Clear space wanted on each side for sliding snow, how the hoop sheds snow at its shallow peak, and whether gutters fit.
  • The wide door: its real opening and clear width, one leaf or two, which way it swings, the sill, whether both ends can have it, and its price against the standard door.
  • Whether crop strings or trellis loads can hang from the hoops or the 7 ft truss chord, and how much weight they can carry.
  • Ventilation for 76 ft: are the two end vents all the kit has? What do they advise for the sides or the ridge, how are vents 7 to 10 ft up opened and held, and can more vent bays be framed into an end wall?
  • A quote for two bays: freight to Prairie, lead time, what the kit includes (end walls, doors, fasteners, trim), installation help, and what "typical loads" means for the warranty.

For NRCS and the county

  • Does a 76 ft custom length meet the NRCS high-tunnel practice standard? What records, ranking dates, and approvals come first? Nothing is bought or dug before the contract is approved.
  • Does an unheated tunnel with an earth-floor bed qualify, and does a heater anywhere change that?
  • Will Elmore County confirm in writing that a detached production greenhouse is exempt under Idaho Code 39-4116(5), and what setback or easement check applies, including at the west property line, from the west wall?
