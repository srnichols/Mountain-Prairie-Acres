At a glance

Buy list

Rows are listed south to north and columns east to west, as in the planting map: C1, the west end of each row beside the windbreak, is on the right, and C13, the east end by the driveway, is on the left. Every other row is staggered half a spot. Where it works, same-cultivar trees sit side by side for signage and easy picking. The west end of R1 (on the right) holds the Chestnut crabapple because that end has the fewest pollen partners, and the east end of the apple rows (on the left) holds three different apples for the same reason.

Pollination check

Using the pollination grid's rules: partners are other cultivars within 50 ft that bloom in the same or an adjacent period and are compatible (apples: diploid donors whose flowering group is within one of the tree's own; cherries and pears: the bloom charts on the grid page). Living trees and the other planned trees count. Tart cherries, plums, and peaches are self-fertile and need no partner.

Season fit

Bands are relative to each fruit's own harvest in a cool northern climate (apples: Early before about Sept 10, Mid about Sept 10 to Oct 5, Late about Oct 5 to 25, Very late after Oct 25; see the guide for the others). Unidentified trees, bushes with no band, and trees whose profile gives no band are counted as not banded.

Why these picks

The site

  • About 4,750 ft, nearly flat; Simonton loam with soil pH about 6.2; 12 to 16 in of precipitation, with July and August almost dry (see the water-harvesting plan).
  • In general, bloom-frost risk runs worst to best from apricot to peach, sweet cherry, pear, and apple (a general horticultural ranking, not something measured here). That is why apples carry the plan and the stone-fruit and pear picks are chosen for hardiness: late bloom, frost-tolerant buds, or a hardy species. The apricot is the exception: you asked for apricots, they are the riskiest picks in the plan, and Harcot is planned as a trial. The other apricot spot is now the extra Bing.

What a U-pick orchard needs

  • Fruit people eat off the tree or take home for pies and jam, a harvest that stretches over weeks (the orchard had four early apples), trees small enough to pick without ladders, and few sprays. That points to semi-dwarf trees and disease-resistant cultivars.
  • Sweet cherries and European pears are the harder U-pick crops (birds and splitting; pears are picked hard and ripen off the tree), so they are kept to a modest share of the plan.

Apples: the bought trees and the R1 grafts

  • The 13 bought apples (R4 to R6) fill the thin early band with disease-resistant cultivars and give every tree at least three pollen partners nearby.
  • R1 is the row held for grafting onto the rootstocks you own, so its 13 trees are scionwood, not nursery trees. Its six cultivars favor early and mid-season fruit, strong disease ratings where Purdue's table covers them, and scionwood that nurseries list for 2027. Redfree is vigorous, so two of its three trees go on the EMLA 7 rootstocks; lower-vigor scions such as Pixie Crunch suit the vigorous M-111.
  • One of the 14 rootstocks you own is left over after the 13 grafts. Keep it as a spare for a graft that fails or a later replacement.

Cherries at the east end of R9 and R10, plus one Bing in R11 (12 spots)

  • All twelve are ordered and not planted yet. Six are tart and self-fertile: four Surefire and two more Montmorency. In the Intermountain West, sweet cherry is the main host of bacterial canker (Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide), and nearly all of the orchard's sweet cherries are flagged as positive (see the canker note below). WSU's work lists Sweetheart, Bing, Staccato, Royal Ann, and Van as susceptible cultivars and Mazzard and Colt as the more resistant rootstocks.
  • The six sweet ones are not self-fertile. Two Black Pearl on Mazzard go at R9-C2, beside the living Bing trees and about 22 ft from Lapins, and at R9-C10. Two Burgundy Pearl on Mazzard go at R9-C9 and R9-C11, one on each side of that east Black Pearl. Burgundy Pearl is the same incompatibility group as Bing, so Bing cannot pollinate it; the Black Pearl between the two trees is the partner. A third Black Tartarian is at R10-C10, across from that Black Pearl. The twelfth tree is a Bing at R11-C4, about 34 ft from Lapins, because the cherry rows had no twelfth hole. The Chinese (Mormon) apricot trial comes off the plan. Stella does not pollinate Bing in this chart. Cummins calls the pearl cherries canker resistant; that is a nursery claim, not a trial. Birds need netting. Treat the new sweet cherries as a trial before copying the mix.

Plums and a Seckel pear (5 spots)

  • You first asked for four plums, two apricots, and four peaches in the shared stone-fruit rows R11 and R12. On October 28 the two Mount Royal spots, R11-C7 and R12-C7, became peaches, so the new plums are Stanley at R12-C3 and the Damson at R12-C4. Texas A&M AgriLife says bacterial canker can affect all stone fruit but cherries, peaches, and apricots are the most susceptible, so the remaining plums are still the safer stone fruit. Both are self-fertile European types, because the pollination rules cannot pair plums and the Japanese and hybrid plums need a partner the orchard does not have. The three Italian plums stay together at R11-C6, R12-C5, and R12-C6 after your October 18 move.
  • Stanley is a favorite for U-pick but Penn State lists it as very susceptible to black knot (Damson is listed with some resistance), so the plan has one Stanley, not three, in the lowest plum spot, where its late bloom helps it miss spring frost. Wild chokecherry and plum within about 500 ft make black knot worse; prune knots out in winter.
  • Seckel is a small, very sweet, fire-blight-resistant pear that ripens in September, with a mid-season bloom that overlaps Comice. It has fewer partners nearby than most trees here (only Comice at 18 ft), which is a known weak point.

Apricot at R11-C5 (1 spot)

  • Harcot is self-pollinating, so it needs no partner. Stark Bro's calls it frost-hardy and late-blooming, with good tolerance to bacterial spot and perennial canker. It takes the higher of the open gap spots that were not held for plums, in R11, which lidar puts about 0.7 ft above R12 at the same columns. Cold air drains downhill, so the higher row should see a little less of it (not measured on your lot). It sits beside the five apricots you already have (R11-C1 to R11-C3 and R12-C1 to R12-C2), so the apricot block stays together. R11-C4, the other high gap, is the extra Bing.
  • Harcot is the sweet, juicy, rich-flavored apricot, ripe in mid July. You already have a Giant Tilton (R11-C3; Utah State's entry for plain Tilton calls it very hardy and resistant to late frost), a Harcot (R12-C1), a Chinese (Mormon), two Moorparks, and one unidentified apricot, so this adds a second Harcot to compare rather than a second Chinese (Mormon).
  • Treat the apricot as a trial. It is the stone fruit most hurt by spring frost, and nothing measured on your lot shows how its cold air behaves (the site climate page lists this among the things still to check). Log first bloom, full bloom, and any frost damage on it next spring.

Peaches at R11-C7, R11-C10, R11-C12, R12-C7, R12-C8, and R12-C11 (6 spots)

  • Three peaches (J.H. Hale, Canadian Harmony, and Blazing Star) died in their pots before planting, so they say nothing about how peaches do on the site. Every one had been flagged for bacterial canker, which is why the peach picks lean on cold hardiness and disease resistance, not on novelty. All six picks are self-fertile, so no pollination pairing is needed.
  • Intrepid is the best all-around peach for the categories that bind this site: bud hardiness, a finish before an early September frost, firmer fruit than Reliance, and bacterial spot resistance. NC State's patent (PP12357) records about a 40 percent crop at Sandhills in 1996, when the commercial varieties in those plots set nothing after six freezing nights in flower, and it ripens about two weeks after Redhaven. One was already ordered at R11-C10. The two Mount Royal spots, R11-C7 and R12-C7, are two more of the same tree, ordered October 28 on Roots to Fruits WZTA0LZIJ, seedling, Field Ready, charged $149. Contender is the flavor pick, not the season pick: Dave Wilson calls it very flavorful and firm, Utah State lists it as frost tolerant and ripe in mid to late August, and Kentucky's HO-6 table puts it 16 to 20 days after Redhaven. You already have a living Contender at R11-C8 that was not flagged for canker, plus one ordered at R11-C12, so these two spots do not add a third and fourth of the later peach.
  • Harrow Diamond (R12-C11) fills an earlier window: the University of Kentucky lists it about 11 days before Redhaven, rated Good for hardiness and bacterial spot resistance. Contender (R11-C12) is the hardy mid-season tree with the best published flavor: Kentucky rates its hardiness above Harrow Diamond's, and both above Redhaven's, and Utah State lists it as very cold hardy and tolerant of spring frost. Together with the Reliance and Redhaven you already have, they spread the harvest over several weeks.
  • Reliance (R12-C8) is the fourth, the safe early tree: very cold-hardy, with very hardy fruit buds and late bloom (zone 4 to 8), planted beside the Reliance at R12-C9. It is soft and bruises easily, and Gurney's says newer hardy peaches such as Contender taste better, so it is the reliable choice, not the best-tasting one.
  • Ask for Lovell or Bailey peach seedling rootstock. Penn State's summary of the NC-140 rootstock trials found peach rootstocks survived best, while non-peach and hybrid rootstocks suffered cold injury in the Midwest and bacterial canker in the Southeast.

Bacterial canker in the stone fruit

Suggestions not followed

  • Encore peach topped the first round of research for being the latest ripener. It ripens in fall, after Elberta, which is already a gamble here, so Intrepid replaces it.
  • Krymsk 86 rootstock was suggested for Intrepid. UC Davis lists it as a peach and myrobalan plum hybrid, and Penn State's summary of the NC-140 trials found hybrid rootstocks more prone to cold injury and canker than peach seedling stocks, so the plan says Lovell or Bailey.
  • Gisela 6 cherry rootstock was suggested for the sweet cherries. The Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide and WSU both list Gisela 6 among the most canker-susceptible rootstocks and Mazzard among the least, so the ordered pearl cherries are on Mazzard.

Ripening watch: apples you already have

Braeburn at R2-C2 belongs on this watch list too: its band is Late, but it is disease-susceptible and wants a long warm fall. Golden Delicious, Idared, Red Delicious, and Mutsu are less likely to be a problem: the Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide's apple table, written for Utah, lists them as cold hardy with September to October harvests. Prairie has a shorter season than most of Utah, so treat that as encouraging, not proof.
