Pollination check

Using the pollination grid's rules: partners are other cultivars within 50 ft that bloom in the same or an adjacent period and are compatible (apples: diploid donors whose flowering group is within one of the tree's own; cherries and pears: the bloom charts on the grid page). Living trees and the other planned trees count. Tart cherries, plums, and peaches are self-fertile and need no partner.

Season fit

Bands are relative to each fruit's own harvest in a cool northern climate (apples: Early before about Sept 10, Mid about Sept 10 to Oct 5, Late about Oct 5 to 25, Very late after Oct 25; see the guide for the others). Unidentified trees, bushes with no band, and trees whose profile gives no band are counted as not banded.

Why these picks

The site

  • About 4,750 ft, nearly flat; Simonton loam with soil pH about 6.2; 12 to 16 in of precipitation, with July and August almost dry (see the water-harvesting plan).
  • NRCS lists only 60 to 100 frost-free days for this soil's setting. The four NOAA stations serving Elmore County share an 80-day season, and the longest single station allows 160 days; the nearest similar-elevation station (Hill City, 5,100 ft) has a median season of 111 days. Best estimate here: 70 to 110 days and USDA zone 5a to 5b. Sources disagree and the USDA map was not checked for this exact point, so treat these as estimates, not readings.
  • In general, bloom-frost risk runs worst to best from apricot to peach, sweet cherry, pear, and apple (a general horticultural ranking, not something measured here). That is why apples carry the plan and the stone-fruit and pear picks are chosen for hardiness: late bloom, frost-tolerant buds, or a hardy species.

What a U-pick orchard needs

  • Fruit people eat off the tree or take home for pies and jam, a harvest that stretches over weeks (the orchard had four early apples), trees small enough to pick without ladders, and few sprays. That points to semi-dwarf trees and disease-resistant cultivars.
  • Sweet cherries and European pears are the harder U-pick crops (birds and splitting; pears are picked hard and ripen off the tree), so they are kept to a modest share of the plan.

Apples: the bought trees and the R1 grafts

  • The 13 bought apples (R4 to R6) fill the thin early band with disease-resistant cultivars and give every tree at least three pollen partners nearby.
  • R1 is the row held for grafting onto the rootstocks you own, so its 13 trees are scionwood, not nursery trees. Its six cultivars favor early and mid-season fruit, strong disease ratings where Purdue's table covers them, and scionwood that nurseries list for 2027. Redfree is vigorous, so two of its three trees go on the EMLA 7 rootstocks; lower-vigor scions such as Pixie Crunch suit the vigorous M-111.
  • One of the 14 rootstocks you own is left over after the 13 grafts. Keep it as a spare for a graft that fails or a later replacement.

Cherries at the east end of R9 and R10 (10 spots)

  • Seven of the ten are tart or tart-type (Surefire, Meteor, Carmine Jewel). They are hardy, bloom late, and are self-fertile. In the Intermountain West, sweet cherry is the main host of bacterial canker (Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide), and nearly all of the orchard's sweet cherries are flagged as positive (see the canker note below). WSU's work lists Sweetheart, Bing, Staccato, Royal Ann, and Van as susceptible cultivars and Mazzard and Colt as the more resistant rootstocks.
  • The three sweet ones (two Skeena and one WhiteGold) are self-fertile, so they need no partner this far from the Bing trees, and should be bought on Mazzard. No trial rates either for canker, and WhiteGold's resistance is only a nursery claim. Treat the 10 cherries as a two- or three-winter trial before copying the mix.

Plums and a Seckel pear (8 spots)

  • Texas A&M AgriLife says bacterial canker can affect all stone fruit but cherries, peaches, and apricots are the most susceptible, so plums are the safer addition to the shared stone-fruit rows. All three plum cultivars are self-fertile European types, because the pollination rules cannot pair plums and the Japanese and hybrid plums need a partner the orchard does not have. The orchard has only the three Italian plums.
  • Stanley is a favorite for U-pick but Penn State lists it as very susceptible to black knot (Damson is listed with some resistance). Wild chokecherry and plum within about 500 ft make black knot worse; prune knots out in winter.
  • Seckel is a small, very sweet, fire-blight-resistant pear that ripens in September, with a mid-season bloom that overlaps Comice. It has fewer partners nearby than most trees here (only Comice at 18 ft), which is a known weak point.

Bacterial canker in the stone fruit

Suggestions not followed

  • Encore peach topped the first round of research for being the latest ripener. It ripens in fall, after Elberta, which is already a gamble here, so Intrepid replaces it.
  • Gisela 6 cherry rootstock was suggested for Black Gold. The Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide and WSU both list Gisela 6 among the most canker-susceptible rootstocks and Mazzard among the least, so the plan says Mazzard.

Ripening watch: apples you already have

These apples ripen very late, after about Oct 25 in a cool northern climate. With 70 to 110 frost-free days they may not ripen reliably here. Watch them for the first few crops; any that cannot ripen before the hard freeze can be top-worked (grafted over) to an early or mid-season cultivar.

Braeburn at R2-C2 belongs on this watch list too: its band is Late, but it is disease-susceptible and wants a long warm fall. Golden Delicious, Idared, Red Delicious, and Mutsu are less likely to be a problem: the Intermountain Tree Fruit Production Guide's apple table, written for Utah, lists them as cold hardy with September to October harvests. Prairie has a shorter season than most of Utah, so treat that as encouraging, not proof.
